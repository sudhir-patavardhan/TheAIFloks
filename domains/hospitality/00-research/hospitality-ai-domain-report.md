# Domain Report: AI in Hospitality

*Research phase output — 00-research, 2026-09-10. Synthesizes `investment-landscape.md` (sources #1–62), `industry-challenges.md` (#71–100) and `unsolved-problems.md` (#101–130); every claim below carries a source number (#) traceable to `sources.md`. ## Executive summary

Every hotel group, online travel agency and quick-service chain of scale shipped the same standardized AI layer in 2025–2026, available identically to every property or customer: conversational hotel search on the brand's own surfaces (Marriott's Ask Bonvoy, Hilton's AI Planner, IHG, Wyndham, Choice — five equivalent launches in one half-year, #1, #5–#10, #16), a brand presence inside ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini (Accor first, then Wyndham, IHG, Hilton — #7, #15, #18), LLM customer-service agents (Booking, Agoda, Expedia and Airbnb all reporting the identical "double-digit decline in cost per booking," Airbnb resolving ~45% of inquiries without a human — #21–#27), vendor revenue-management engines (IDeaS serves 16,000 clients including Marriott, IHG *and* Accor, #42), guest-messaging AI (Canary sells it to 20,000+ hotels, and Wyndham brands the same capability "proprietary" Connect+, #15, #39), and drive-thru voice AI (three chains, two vendors: McDonald's and Wendy's on Google, Taco Bell on NVIDIA — #48–#52). No chain has disclosed a conversion lift for any AI planner; Expedia states natural-language search "does not currently drive conversion" (#25). J.P. Morgan's own "AI payoff" note carries a "brand differentiation" risk flag (#20).

The dollars are going somewhere else: substrate. Marriott's ~$1.1B 2026 investment plan puts a third-plus into replatforming its property-management, reservation and loyalty systems, reimbursed over time by owners (#2, #3, #20); IHG is finishing an eight-year rebuild with cloud PMS in 4,000 hotels and an AI revenue-management system across its whole estate (#10); Wyndham cites $450M+ since 2018 (#15); the largest venture round in the sector is Mews's $300M for a PMS (#37). The chains say it out loud — "model-agnostic AI chassis" (#4), "AI-ready technology stack" (#9), "common technology foundation" (#50). Nobody of scale is betting on a proprietary model.

The assets that pass the exclusive-data test all predate generative AI. Bonvoy's 271–295M members produce 75% of Marriott's U.S. & Canada room nights (#1, #77, #105); Hilton is "80% plus direct" and locates its moat in "control of rate inventory availability," with AI helping "on the margin" (#7, #106); IHG One Rewards covers 67% of room nights (#10). Booking's connected trip grows at twice its base and its Genius tiers now cover roughly half of room nights (#21, #87). Airbnb's moat is a 100%-verified two-sided identity graph (#26). In QSR, Yum's CFO attributes Taco Bell's 900-store voice-AI rollout not to the AI but to the owned Byte platform and its 61% first-party digital mix (#50, #51, #124), and Domino's runs DomOS on 2.5B orders of its own timing data (#59). In every case AI is currently a *consumer* of the asset — search grounding, personalization, cost reduction — not yet a demonstrated compounder of it.

Underneath sit two AI-agnostic structural roots on opposite sides of the operator's P&L (`industry-challenges.md`). Revenue side: the operator does not own its demand. OTAs take 63.4% of independent bookings at double the cancellation rate of direct (#85); Booking earned $26.9B revenue and $9.9B EBITDA in 2025 while spending $8.2B on marketing to buy the demand it resells (#86, #87); franchise fees grow faster than room revenue (#78); NYC's delivery-commission cap was raised to an effective 43% (#99). The EU's parity-clause ban did not end parity — conversion-based ranking now penalizes undercutting without a clause (#88, #89), and 10,783 European hotels are suing (#91). Cost side: the product is physical and human-delivered, and its labor pool is policy-constrained — wages per occupied room +12.8% in 2025 and +21.1% in Q4 (#73), quits at 3.5% vs 1.9% economy-wide (#74), one-third immigrant workforce against a 66,000 H-2B cap (#75). Every shipped AI product touches the screen-based work *around* these roots (the call center, the search box, the RFP inbox) and neither root itself.

The agentic layer is following the ad-servers script pre-emptively. OpenAI shelved native Instant Checkout in March 2026 and pushed transactions back into Expedia's and Booking's apps (#33, #109); Google's AI Mode hotel booking launched September 2026 on an open protocol (UCP for Lodging, interoperable with A2A/MCP) with the chain or OTA retained as merchant of record and ten partners that are all chains and OTAs — no independents (#30–#32, #110); Booking reports LLM traffic "below 1% of room nights" (#108). Nobody expects to own the rail, and the coordination layer is standardizing around the incumbents' inventory rather than disintermediating them.

## How AI is actually being used — by layer

**Hotel-group platforms and loyalty.** A three-part playbook at every major chain: conversational search on owned surfaces, presence inside third-party LLMs, and an internal program organized by persona (Marriott guest/associate/owner, #4; IHG acquisition/performance/efficiency, #10; Hilton efficiency/distribution/guest, #7). The most quantified outcome is Wyndham's Connect+: 25% faster handle time and ~300bps incremental direct contribution across 1,100 hotels (#15) — the same capability Canary sells to 20,000 hotels (#39). Hilton, two quarters after launching its planner, has disclosed no usage or conversion data (#9). Institutional-capability candidates: Marriott's self-built chassis and "agentic mesh" (still "pulling into the players' parking lot," #3, #4) and Hyatt's internally built agentic platforms and RFP tooling on 1.5M RFPs a year, whose CEO measures success by "absorption" (#11, #12) — the Individual-Invention-to-institution transition attempted in the open.

**OTA/distribution and agentic booking.** The most consistent outcome in the domain is customer-service cost falling double-digit at all four platforms at once (#21–#27) — a baseline by definition. Trip planners (Penny, Booking.com discovery, TripGenie, Expedia's Layla acquisition) carry no disclosed conversion lift (#22–#25, #29); Expedia's candid framing is "60% more information regarding traveler intent" but no conversion (#25). Search engines fell from 51% to 36% as travelers' most-used research resource in ~18 months (#34), and answer-engine optimization is Expedia's fastest-growing channel (#24) — the new SEO, and every seller does it. Trip.com is exposing inventory to third-party agents via MCP (#29).

**Revenue management.** Vendor RMS is the textbook shared engine (#42, #43); Cloudbeds sells a "Signals" model to independents (#35). The one candidate for more is IHG's estate-wide AI RMS on IHG's own demand data, claiming "revenue uplift and market share gains" — unquantified (#10). Forecasting's limit is demonstrated by the forecasters: STR/Tourism Economics' 2026 U.S. RevPAR forecast moved from +0.6% to +2.8% to +4.4% within six months, the upgrade attributed to one-off World Cup demand and "ongoing conflict with Iran" as a live variable (#83, #113, #114). A property's pricing engine learns a faster booking curve; it does not see the shock.

**Guest service and messaging.** Canary (#39), Wyndham Connect+ (#15), Accor's concierge resolving 40% of queries (#19), Booking's partner messaging (#22), and Oracle bundling an assistant into OPERA Cloud at no extra cost (#41). Available identically to every property.

**Operations and labor.** Sparse and vendor-reported: Actabl's predictive labor module cut overtime share 13% across 100+ beta hotels (#44); Accor *expects* 30–60 minutes saved per employee per day from its PMS assistant (#19). The one hard productivity number in the operating data is not AI: room-attendant minutes per occupied room fell 4.3% to Q1 2026, attributed to scheduling discipline (#116). Deployed robotics is explicitly augmentation — TechForce's TIM-E hauls linen for ~$150/day so housekeepers cleaning 16–18 rooms stop carrying "several hundred pounds" (#119); Pudu's fully robot-run hotel in Shenzhen opens in 2027 with no disclosed staffing (#120). Hyatt cut 30% of U.S. customer-service staff (#14). Labor effects land in the call center, not the guest room.

**Restaurants and QSR.** Drive-thru voice AI: Taco Bell at 900+ stores (#49, #50), McDonald's ArchIQ at five test stores after the cancelled IBM pilot, with Google Edge hardware being pre-installed nationwide before any confirmed outcome (#48), Wendy's targeting 500+ (#52). The only independent measurement — Intouch Insight's 2025 mystery shop — put AI order accuracy at 83% vs 89% for humans, with employees intervening in roughly a quarter of interactions (#123); 80% of consumers prefer a human order-taker (#53). Kitchen automation is funded through shared vendors (Chipotle and Cava both into Hyphen, #57) or divested — Sweetgreen sold its Infinite Kitchen unit to Wonder for $186M (#58) while reporting >700bps labor savings at established IK stores against comparable sales down 12.8% (#126). Chipotle's hiring bot (12→4 days, #55) is a purchased product; Starbucks' Green Dot Assist runs on Azure OpenAI (#54). Delivery platforms shipped near-identical cart assistants four months apart (#60, #61).

**Internal engineering and staff productivity.** Airbnb ~60% of code AI-written (#26, #28); Booking and Expedia citing faster shipping (#22, #25); Yum with 400+ internal agents (#50); AccorGPT saving 2.5 hrs/week for 7,000 users (#19). Universal and non-differentiating.

## Where the investment is concentrated

Of ~21 initiative categories, 13 are Process Automation at scale, 3 Personal Productivity, 2 structural, and only 3 Institutional Capability — and those three (loyalty graphs plus rate/inventory control, cross-vertical transaction graphs, first-party QSR platforms) are data assets built before the generative-AI wave. See `investment-landscape.md` for the full table and the skeptical test applied to each candidate. Individual Invention is faint (Hyatt's internal builders, Marriott's chassis) and, where it exists, is being deliberately institutionalized. Nothing found is an AI-native institutional capability with a disclosed compounding outcome.

## The unsolved problems, tested

Seven long-standing problems were run through the binding-constraint test (`unsolved-problems.md`). Six came back **automation in disguise**: guest personalization at scale (constraint is integration and ownership — only 24% of hotels have integrated core systems, and the OTA keeps the guest record by design, #101, #88); direct-vs-OTA distribution under the agentic layer (aggregation relocated to Google and OpenAI in partnership with the existing aggregators, not removed, #108–#111); total revenue management under exogenous shocks (#113, #114); labor that doesn't scale (augmentation next to an unchanged housekeeping cycle, #119–#121); the independent hotel's disadvantage (the purchased stack closes the purchasable gap for everyone, the agentic surfaces widen the non-purchasable one, #103, #110); and short-term-rental trust and safety (an arms race — Airbnb now bans AI-generated evidence by policy because detection can't keep up, and the largest anti-discrimination gain came from hiding profile photos, #127–#129).

Two candidates survive. **Genuine invention candidate:** owned kitchen automation — Sweetgreen's Infinite Kitchen is the only case in the evidence base where the reported gain (>700bps labor) targets the actual binding constraint (assembly throughput) rather than the interface around it; provisional because self-reported and because the parent's demand is collapsing regardless (#126). **Open opportunity:** a hotel-owned, collective agent-facing distribution layer (inventory, content, identity, merchant of record) for independents — the protocols now standardizing make it cheaper than ever, chains and OTAs are each building it for themselves, and nobody is building it for the 63% of independent bookings that flow through OTAs (#103, #109, #110). This is the hospitality analogue of the ad-servers consortium proposal.

## Falsification check

Looked for a place where purchased automation *is* the moat. Three candidates: (1) simultaneous identical CS-cost declines at Booking, Expedia and Airbnb — a rising baseline by definition (#108, #111, #112); (2) Taco Bell's 900-store voice AI — resolves in the framework's favor, since Yum locates the value in owned Byte, not the purchasable vendor (#124); (3) the strongest remaining candidate, which `02-analysis` must adjudicate: **the franchise network as deployment rail.** Wyndham pushed Connect+ to 1,100 hotels, IHG its RMS to 6,000, Yum voice AI to 900 stores in months, with owners paying via fee reimbursement (#2, #10, #15, #50). If the advantage is the ability to push a purchased capability across an estate faster than rivals, the moat is the franchise system's structure — real, durable, and not an AI capability. That is consistent with the framework but is the domain's most honest complication.

## Working thesis (to be grilled)

Hospitality's durable AI value sits where it did in ad tech and fashion — in genuinely exclusive, consented data (the chains' loyalty graphs and inventory control; Booking's connected trip; Airbnb's verified graph; Yum's first-party platform) — and every one of those assets was built by loyalty economics, franchise contracts and platform scale *before* the LLM cycle. Purchased AI is currently a consumer of those assets, shaving cost off the screen-based work around the industry's two real constraints (demand owned upstream; labor physically bound), and touching neither. The agentic booking layer is standardizing as an open protocol around the incumbents' inventory from day one, which reinforces the chain/OTA duopoly and excludes independents — making a collective, hotel-owned agent-facing layer the domain's one open opportunity. The falsification pressure point is whether the franchise deployment rail is itself the moat.

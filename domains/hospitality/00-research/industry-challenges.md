# Hospitality: Structural Challenges (AI-Agnostic)

Researched deliberately without treating AI as the lens — before testing any constraint against AI, per the framework's "hunt the impossible" step. The point is to name the industry's real structural problems on their own terms, not to reverse-engineer problems that happen to be shaped like whatever AI tools are for sale. Primary lens is lodging (global hotel groups, independents, OTAs and the hotel-tech stack); secondary lens is restaurants and food service. Cited claims reference `sources.md` by number (#71–#100); a few points are general industry-structure background rather than claims on the sourced list — those are marked **[background, uncited]** and should not be treated as sourced facts. Research date: 2026-09-10.

## The finding: two root structural conflicts, not one — and they compound

Ad-serving had one root conflict (the referee is also a bidder). Hospitality does not reduce to a single conflict, but it does reduce to two, and they sit on opposite sides of the operator's P&L:

1. **On the revenue side: the operator does not own its demand.** The customer relationship — and a rising share of every dollar the guest pays — is held by intermediaries above the operator: OTAs and delivery platforms (for independents and restaurants) and brand/franchise systems (for the ~two-thirds to four-fifths of branded rooms that are franchised). Every one of those intermediaries is structurally incentivized to grow its share of the guest's spend, and each has done so faster than the operator's own revenue has grown.
2. **On the cost side: the product is physical, on-site, and human-delivered, and the labor supply that delivers it is structurally constrained.** A room can be sold online but it can only be cleaned in person. Hospitality cannot offshore its core cost, is unusually dependent on immigrant and young-adult labor pools that are shrinking or policy-blocked, and has been paying for that with double-digit wage-cost growth per occupied room.

Most of what the industry describes as its "challenges" — margin compression, the owner-vs-brand fight, OTA dependence, the personalization gap despite enormous loyalty databases, the fragility of independent restaurants — is best read as the two roots meeting in the middle. Two further forces (capital cost and the political license to operate) are genuinely independent and are treated separately below.

### 1. Demand is owned upstream: the distribution-and-brand intermediation squeeze — root cause (revenue side)

The core structural fact: the entities that hold the guest relationship are not the entities that own the asset, employ the staff, or bear the operating cost — and their take grows faster than the operator's revenue.

**Independent hotels and OTAs.**
- OTAs' share of independent-hotel bookings reached 63.4% globally in 2025, approaching 80% in some markets; OTA bookings cancel at 21.8% versus 10.6% for direct bookings, so the intermediated demand is also lower-quality demand (#85, vendor dataset of 90M bookings, flagged accordingly). In the same year independents' global RevPAR fell 5.4% (ADR −5.8%) while the branded, loyalty-driven U.S. market grew (#85 vs. #77).
- Booking Holdings earned $26.9B of revenue and more than $9.9B of adjusted EBITDA (+20%) in 2025 on more than 1.2 billion room nights, and spent $8.2B on marketing — up from $7.3B — to buy the demand it resells to hotels (#86, #87). Its own 10-K names the counter-move it fears: travel service providers "may offer lower prices through direct or AI-enabled channels" (#86). Its Genius loyalty tiers 2–3 are now over 30% of its active base and roughly half of room nights (#87) — i.e., the OTA is building its *own* loyalty layer on top of the hotels' inventory.
- Regulators have found the intermediation abusive and are still litigating it. Spain's CNMC fined Booking.com €413.2M in July 2024 for imposing unfair conditions on Spanish hotels and restricting rival OTAs; the fine is suspended pending appeal (#90). The EU designated Booking a DMA gatekeeper (May 2024) and banned wide and narrow parity clauses from November 14, 2024 (#88). Following the CJEU's September 2024 judgment that Booking's parity clauses breached EU competition law, 10,783 European hotels have joined a collective damages action filed at the Amsterdam District Court in January 2026 covering 2004–2024, with a third expansion to ~18,000 hotels planned for autumn 2026 (#91). Booking, for its part, points to a March 2026 Amsterdam ruling in the German hotels' case that hotels "have still not provided evidence" that pre-2016 parity clauses restricted competition (#92) — the liability question is genuinely open, not settled.
- The parity ban has not ended parity in practice. At Booking's own DMA compliance workshop, hoteliers asked whether its price-quality score and sponsored-benefit programs were "measures with equivalent effect" to the banned clauses, and Booking refused to share guest email addresses before arrival (#88, notes). Trade analysis in June 2026 describes the mechanism that replaced the clause: conversion-based ranking means a hotel that undercuts Booking on its own site "converts worse" on Booking and "drifts down the page on its own" — "the platform needs no clause demanding parity; its ranking already makes undercutting expensive" (#89).

**Branded hotels and their franchisors.** The same shape recurs one layer up: for branded hotels the demand-owner is the brand, and the brand's fee grows faster than the owner's revenue.
- Hilton's portfolio is 79.3% franchised by rooms; Marriott's is 66.5% franchised, with only 0.8% owned or leased (#76, #77). Marriott Bonvoy's 271M members generated 75% of U.S. & Canada room nights and 68% globally in 2025; Marriott's gross fee revenue was $5.44B (#77). The brand, not the owner, holds the guest.
- Across CBRE's sample, franchise-related fees rose 3.5% from 2023 to 2024 against rooms revenue growth of 2.7%, driven by loyalty-program costs (+3.9%) and reservation/marketing assessments (+3.8%) — the components tied most directly to the brand's ownership of demand. Fees now split roughly evenly among royalty, marketing/reservation, and loyalty charges, and CBRE notes fees have "typically grown at a pace greater than the rise in hotel rooms revenue" over the years (#78).
- Owners have organized around this. AAHOA — whose ~20,000 members own roughly 60% of U.S. hotels — maintains its "12 Points of Fair Franchising," most recently adding a change-of-control exit right after brand mergers (#79). Owner litigation has, however, mostly not succeeded: the 2020 RICO/kickback suit by dozens of Choice Hotels franchisees went to arbitration, the arbitrator rejected all claims, and a federal court confirmed the award for Choice in September 2025 (#80). Hilton's 10-K describes the friction from the brand side: it provides owners "property improvement plans that must be satisfied to keep hotels in our system" and acknowledges owners "may be able to terminate the contracts under certain circumstances" (#76).

**Restaurants and delivery platforms.** The third instance, with the thinnest margins underneath.
- Third-party delivery commissions run 15–30% of order value on the platforms' standard tiers **[background, uncited as a single figure; consistent with the NYC cap structure in #99]** against an industry in which 42% of operators reported their restaurant was not profitable last year and more than 9 in 10 cite food, labor, insurance, energy and swipe fees as significant challenges (#98). DoorDash's Marketplace GOV grew 27% in 2025 on $13.7B of revenue, with a Q4 net revenue margin of 13.3% of GOV (#100).
- New York City's pandemic-era 23% commission cap was raised in April 2025 to an effective 43% (15% delivery + 5% marketing + 3% processing + 20% "enhanced services") as the price of settling DoorDash, Grubhub, Uber Eats and Relay's lawsuits against the city (#99). DoorDash's 10-K lists "regulations impacting the commission rates we charge to merchants" among its risks and notes merchants may "renegotiate commission rates, or cease operations" (#100) — the platform and the regulator, not the restaurant, set the terms.

**Why this is root, not a symptom:** it is the incentive structure the industry's revenue model is built on, and it is the same structure at three different scales. Whoever owns the guest relationship — OTA, delivery app, or brand — can price its access to that guest, and will grow that price as long as the operator has no cheaper way to reach demand. The regulatory record (CNMC, DMA, CJEU, the Amsterdam action, the NYC cap fight) shows this is contested at the level of market structure, not customer service; the post-DMA persistence of de facto parity shows that removing the contract clause did not remove the underlying leverage.

### 2. The product is human-delivered and the labor supply is structurally constrained — root cause (cost side)

The core structural fact: hospitality's largest controllable cost cannot be relocated, and the pools it draws from are shrinking or policy-blocked.

- 65% of U.S. hotels reported staffing shortages in AHLA's December 2024–January 2025 survey (9% "severely"), 71% had openings they could not fill despite active recruiting, with 6–7 open positions per property and housekeeping (38%) and front desk (26%) the hardest to fill (#71). Fourteen months later, more than half of surveyed properties were still "somewhat or severely understaffed," labor costs were the number-two financial pressure (65%) behind supplies (71%), and 70% were raising wages to recruit (#72).
- The cost of that recruiting shows up directly in unit economics: wages per occupied room rose 12.8% in 2025, from $42.82 to $48.32, with hours per occupied room up 4.4% — more hours *and* more dollars per hour. In Q4 2025 alone, wages per occupied room rose 21.1% year-over-year (full-service hotels: +23.8% to $73.53) and GOP margin fell 3.3 points to 36.0% (#73, vendor benchmark, flagged accordingly).
- Turnover is easing from its post-2020 peak but remains roughly double the economy's: the accommodation and food services quits rate was 3.5% in July 2026 (down from 4.7% a year earlier) against 1.9% for all nonfarm employment (#74).
- The labor pool itself is the constraint. Roughly one-third of the ~8M workers directly employed in travel and hospitality are immigrants (U.S. Travel/AHLA figures), the H-2B seasonal visa cap remains 66,000, and U.S. hotels carried roughly one million unfilled openings in 2025 (#75). The NRA's 2026 report separately flags "the shrinking 16-to-24-year-old population" as a long-term labor-force constraint requiring immigration reform (#98). Hilton's 10-K names "labor shortages, which could restrict our ability to efficiently operate or grow our business," and reports 45% of its U.S. workforce covered by collective bargaining agreements (#76). Union contracts settled in 2025 in several U.S. cities raised housekeeper wages toward $24–30/hour by 2027–2028 while cutting daily room quotas **[background, uncited]**.

**Why this is root, not a symptom:** a hotel room and a restaurant meal are produced at the point of consumption by people physically present. Unlike almost every other industry facing wage inflation, hospitality cannot offshore, cannot ship the work to a cheaper labor market, and cannot substantially reduce the human component without changing the product it sells. The constraint is the intersection of that physical fact with demographic and immigration-policy realities that the industry does not control.

### 3. Margin compression under revenue that grows slower than both intermediary fees and labor cost — consequence of §1 and §2

- The top-line has recovered without pricing power flowing through to operators: U.S. RevPAR grew only 0.7% at Marriott's U.S. & Canada hotels in 2025 (#77); CoStar/Tourism Economics' 2026 U.S. forecast started the year at +0.6% and was upgraded to +2.8% in June and +4.4% in August, but the upgrade was explicitly attributed to one-off World Cup premiums — without the World Cup, June inbound travel would have been the fourth straight monthly decline — and 2027 is forecast at +2.1% (#83).
- Against that, fees to demand-owners grew 3.5% (#78) and wages per occupied room grew 12.8% (#73) in the most recent comparable years. Independents, who pay the OTA rather than the brand, saw RevPAR fall 5.4% globally and 4.4% in the U.S. in 2025 (#85).
- The restaurant version is starker: 42% of operators not profitable in 2025 with sales still growing to a projected $1.55T (#98) — a volume-up, profit-down industry. Casual-dining chains including Hooters (Chapter 11, March 2025, citing rising food and labor costs and falling traffic) illustrate the pattern (#98, notes).

**Why this reads as consequence:** margin compression is simply where the revenue-side root (§1) and the cost-side root (§2) meet. Neither the OTA's take nor the housekeeper's wage is a management failure at the property; they are the two structural facts of the business arriving on the same P&L.

### 4. Fragmented, operator-hostile data and the "personalization gap" despite enormous loyalty databases — consequence of §1, with an operational layer of its own

- The industry has the data in aggregate and lacks it at the property: 271M Bonvoy and 243M Hilton Honors members (#76, #77), yet only 11% of hotel operators report a fully integrated technology stack, 27% run more than seven platforms, 27% spend more than 11 hours per week reconciling data, 91% still rely on manual reporting, and just 15% are "very confident" in the accuracy and timeliness of their operational data (#97, vendor survey, flagged accordingly).
- The fragmentation is not accidental: the guest record is split between the demand-owner and the operator by design. Booking declined at its DMA workshop to share guest email addresses before check-in on the argument that the end user is not "acquired" by the hotel until Booking has been paid (#88, notes); OTA bookings arrive with double the cancellation rate and less guest information than direct ones (#85). The property that serves the guest is often the party that knows least about them.

**Why this reads as consequence:** the operator's inability to know its own guest is downstream of §1 — the intermediary owns the relationship and has every incentive to keep the data with it. The layer that is genuinely operational rather than structural is the on-property tech debt (multiple unintegrated systems, manual reconciliation), which would exist even in a world of 100% direct bookings; that part is addressable and is called out again in the concluding section.

## Two independent structural forces, not downstream of §1 or §2

### A. Capital cost and the supply cycle — independent, and double-edged

- Median U.S. hotel development cost is $213,000 per room (full-service $467,000; luxury $1.6M+); construction-cost inflation ran 4.1% in 2025 and 4.8% in the trailing twelve months to mid-2026 with tariffs on imported metals a named driver; lenders "remain selective, frequently requiring larger equity contributions" (#81). Net supply growth was 0.2% (2023), 0.6% (2024), 0.7% (2025), forecast 0.8%/1.1% for 2026/2027 versus ~1.4% pre-2020 (#81).
- Insurance is *not* uniformly the crisis the industry narrative suggests: after prior-cycle property premium increases that "in some cases, exceeded 150%," hospitality accounts in favorable geographies were achieving 15–25% premium *reductions* by mid-2026, while liability premiums continue to rise on "social inflation, nuclear verdicts and expanding exclusions" and wind/hail-exposed regions remain difficult (#82, broker report). 43% of AHLA's surveyed hoteliers still list insurance premiums as a top financial pressure (#72).

**Why this is listed separately, and why it cuts both ways:** the capital squeeze is macro (rates, construction inflation, tariffs), not a product of hospitality's incentive structure. And constrained new supply is the *main thing propping up* incumbent hotels' pricing power in a slow-demand environment — it hurts developers and helps owners of existing assets. It is a real structural force but not a one-directional "challenge."

### B. The political license to operate: short-term-rental bans, tourist taxes, and overtourism backlash — independent

- Hospitality is the industry whose product consumes a public good — housing stock and neighborhoods — and the public has begun withdrawing consent. Airbnb's 10-K states flatly that New York City's 2023 regulations "resulted in a de facto ban of short-term rental activities" and that "certain cities have passed onerous restrictions" (#93). Three years on, NYC has 3,522 registered hosts against roughly 40,000 listings before Local Law 18, and Airbnb says hosts are forgoing ~$110M a year (#94).
- Spain's Constitutional Court in March 2025 upheld Barcelona's plan to let all 10,000-plus tourist-apartment licenses lapse by 2028 (#95). Barcelona nearly doubled its hotel tax to $10–17 per night in April 2026, tripled its cruise day-visitor levy, and set an explicit ceiling of 16 million visitors against 15.7 million in 2025 under the slogan "not one tourist more" — on top of a hotel-construction moratorium in place since 2017 (#96). Coordinated anti-tourism protests ran across Barcelona, Palma, Lisbon, Genoa, Venice, the Canary Islands and Amsterdam in 2025–2026, with Amsterdam capping overnight stays at 20 million and Venice charging day-trippers (#96, notes).
- Demand itself is politically exposed on the inbound side: international overnight visits to the U.S. fell 5.7% in 2025 with Canadian visits down 25.7% on "diplomatic tensions and border policies," and Oxford Economics attributes the drag explicitly to "policy uncertainty and enforcement actions" (#84).

**Why this is listed separately:** it does not trace to the intermediation or labor roots. It is a distinct conflict between the industry and the communities it operates in — and, notably, it splits the industry: STR bans in New York shift demand toward hotels, while Barcelona's measures hit hotels and rentals alike. It is regulatory and political, not economic, and it is getting stricter, not looser.

## Considered and set aside as background, not top-tier findings

- **Consumer-demand softness and volatility.** Nearly 20% of AHLA's respondents reported 2026 bookings below expectations (#72) and consumer surveys show household caution **[background, uncited]**, but the 2026 forecasts were revised *up* three times (#83). Demand is cyclical background against which the structural problems play out, not a structural problem itself.
- **Hotel-tech vendor consolidation and the cloud-PMS migration.** Real, and relevant to §4's operational layer, but it is an industry *responding* to fragmentation rather than a structural challenge in its own right; it belongs in `investment-landscape.md`.
- **OTA-vs-OTA and platform-vs-search competition** (Expedia vs. Booking, both vs. Google, delivery duopoly shares). Important to the intermediaries' economics; from the operator's seat it changes *which* intermediary owns the demand, not *whether* one does.

## Structural versus operational — for the constraint analysis that follows

This pass should anchor, not be replaced by, the binding-constraint work in `unsolved-problems.md`. Sorting the above by the nature of the constraint (without yet asking what any technology does to it):

**Structural — incentive or regulatory; would persist under any level of operational excellence:**
- §1 Demand ownership by intermediaries (OTAs, delivery platforms, brands), including the post-DMA persistence of de facto parity through ranking. Contested in courts and regulators; not resolvable at the property.
- §2 The labor-supply constraint's *external* half: immigration policy, visa caps, demographics, and the physical, on-site nature of the product.
- A. Capital cost and the supply cycle (macro; double-edged).
- B. The political license to operate (STR bans, tourist taxes, overtourism, inbound-travel policy).
- The asset-light split between the party that sets standards and captures fees (brand) and the party that funds capital and labor (owner) — an incentive structure, not an execution problem.

**Operational — inside the operator's control, at least in principle:**
- Labor *productivity* and scheduling: hours per occupied room rose 4.4% alongside wages (#73); the wage rate is structural, the hours are not.
- On-property technology fragmentation and manual reconciliation (#97): seven-plus systems and 11 hours a week of data wrangling is tech debt, not market structure.
- The direct-booking cost base and conversion — hotels' own websites, rate strategy, and loyalty execution — within whatever ceiling §1 sets.
- Restaurant menu, cost and channel mix within whatever commission structure §1 and the regulators set.

The throughline worth carrying forward: almost every operational problem on the second list sits *inside* a structural constraint on the first. Faster housekeeping scheduling does not change who the housekeeper can be; better data reconciliation does not change who owns the guest record; better direct-booking conversion does not change the ranking penalty for undercutting the OTA. Whether anything in the domain's investment landscape moves a constraint on the first list, or only accelerates work within it, is the question to press hardest in the next research phase — and this document deliberately does not answer it.

# Unsolved Problems in This Domain

Problems long considered intractable. For each: what was the *binding constraint*, and does AI remove it — or just speed up work within it?

Research pass dated 2026-09-10. Source numbers refer to `sources.md` (#101–#130 are this pass). Primary lens is lodging; Problem 6 covers restaurants/food service.

## Problem 1: "Know me like the concierge does" — guest personalization at scale

- **Why it was considered unsolvable:** The luxury-hotel promise (the concierge who remembers your pillow, your allergy, your anniversary) has never survived scale. Twenty years of loyalty programs and CRM projects produced points balances, not recognition. The reason was never a shortage of data — hotels drown in it — but that the data about one guest is split across a property management system, a central reservation system, an OTA that owns the booking relationship, F&B and spa point-of-sale, a loyalty database, and survey tools, each holding a different identity key, often owned by a different legal entity (brand vs. owner vs. franchisee vs. OTA).
- **Binding constraint:** Coordination cost across silos plus a consent/ownership problem — the same shape as the ad-servers identity problem. Only 24% of hotels report fully integrated core systems, 34% manage guest data centrally, 42% run on disconnected systems (#101); 49% of hoteliers say they cannot get at the data needed for revenue and operational decisions (#102); 63% of hotel tech budgets go to maintaining legacy systems that are "not AI-compatible or do not allow for complex data integration" (#107). Independent operators report losing one to two workdays a week to manual reconciliation across systems (#103).
- **Does AI remove the constraint?** Partially at the modeling layer, not at the binding layer. Large language models genuinely make unstructured guest signals (messages, reviews, service requests) readable at scale — a real constraint that used to require human attention. But the evidence from the two largest chains says the personalization layer sits *on top of* an integration and identity problem AI does not touch. Marriott describes 2026 as the year to build a "horizontal, protocol- and model-agnostic AI chassis" across guest, associate and owner personas — and its CIO's stated guardrail is that Marriott "plans not to expose personal data to a model outside of its enterprise" (#104). That is an institution deciding where its data may go, not a model resolving identity. Marriott's first shipped guest-facing product, Ask Bonvoy, is conversational *search* (#105); Hilton's is an Anthropic-powered *planner* (#106). Both are discovery-side automation. Neither company claims that AI has unified the fragmented on-property record. What both companies do own is the consented identity graph: Bonvoy members accounted for 75% of U.S./Canada room nights and 68% globally in 2025 (#105); Hilton says "80% plus of our business is already direct" (#106). That asset predates the LLM era and was built by loyalty economics, not AI.
- **Evidence anyone is attempting real removal:** The vendor category selling "unified guest profiles" (Cloudbeds, Revinate/Hapi, Stayntouch — #102, #103) is, by definition, purchasable by every hotel; the surveys cited above are mostly run by those vendors. The chains' AI-chassis programs (#104, #105) are the closest thing to an institutional attempt, and they are integration programs first, AI programs second.
- **Institutional-capability formulation:** The intelligence has to live in an owned, consented identity graph plus the operational plumbing that writes every touchpoint back to it — which is exactly what a scaled loyalty program plus a brand-controlled reservation stack is. For a chain, the compounding asset is the member graph (#105, #106); the LLM is an access interface on it, precisely as UID2/LiveRamp turned out to be in ad tech. For an independent, there is no such graph to sit an LLM on: 63.4% of independent bookings arrive via OTAs that own the guest relationship (#103), so the "personalization AI" they can buy has structurally less to personalize from.
- **Verdict:** ☑ Automation in disguise, for every "AI personalization" product on sale today — conversational search, message drafting, upsell suggestions — because the fragmentation and ownership constraint is untouched by the model. The only durable formulation found is the chains' consented loyalty graph, and that is an institutional asset built before and independently of AI.

## Problem 2: The direct-vs-OTA problem, now with an agentic layer on top

- **Why it was considered unsolvable:** No individual hotel — and very few chains — can out-market Booking Holdings or Expedia for demand aggregation. The OTA holds the customer, the search budget, and the conversion-optimized funnel; the hotel pays 15–30% for access. Every "book direct" campaign since 2016 has chipped at the margin without changing the structure. In 2025–2026 the question became whether AI agents (ChatGPT, Google AI Mode, the OTAs' own agents) would disintermediate the OTAs, disintermediate the hotels, or both.
- **Binding constraint:** Demand aggregation. Whoever aggregates the traveler's intent at the moment of choice takes the margin — a coordination-and-scale constraint, not a technical one.
- **Does AI remove the constraint?** No. It relocates the aggregator. Three pieces of 2026 evidence point the same way. (i) OpenAI shelved native Instant Checkout in March 2026, less than six months after launch, and moved transactions back into partner apps — Expedia and Booking.com among the first; Expedia's shares rose 12% and Booking's 8% on the day (#109). (ii) Google's AI Mode hotel booking, launched 2026-09-01, is "agentic" in name only — the traveler still picks the room and approves payment — and its launch partners are Booking.com, Expedia, Hotels.com, Priceline, Trip.com, Hilton, Marriott, IHG, Choice and Wyndham, with the hotel or OTA remaining merchant of record; no independent hotels are on the list (#110). (iii) Booking's CEO reported on 2026-08-04 that traffic from large language models "remains below 1% of room nights," while Booking is "one of the first" partners in Google's agentic booking and its customer-service cost per booking is falling at a double-digit rate (#108). Expedia says agentic traffic "remains small" and that it was an early adopter of ChatGPT's ad product (#111). The agentic layer is being built by, with, and on top of the incumbents' inventory and merchant-of-record infrastructure; it does not give a hotel a new way to aggregate demand for itself.
- **Evidence anyone is attempting real removal:** Hilton is the strongest case for a hotel company using AI to hold the customer directly: an Anthropic-powered planner, and its app "opening within" Gemini's and OpenAI's environments (#106). But Hilton's own CEO frames the advantage as "our control of our inventory, our ability to customize the experience" and an 80%-plus direct share that already existed — AI "on the margin, helps." That is the chain's pre-existing loyalty and inventory control extended into a new channel, not a new aggregation mechanism.
- **Institutional-capability formulation:** The only formulation that removes the constraint is one where the hotel's inventory, content and loyalty identity are exposed to every agent through infrastructure the hotel controls, so the agent aggregates *for* the hotel rather than *over* it. The chains are building that (#105, #106). For independents, the equivalent would be a shared, hotel-owned agent-facing endpoint and merchant-of-record layer — see Problem 5.
- **Verdict:** ☑ Automation in disguise. Every shipped "agentic booking" product to date (#108–#111) is a richer discovery interface on unchanged OTA/chain plumbing — the exact pattern the ad-servers analysis found in AdCP/ARTF. The aggregation constraint is not removed; it is being re-aggregated by Google and OpenAI in partnership with the existing aggregators.

## Problem 3: Total revenue management and forecasting under exogenous shocks

- **Why it was considered unsolvable:** Revenue management optimizes room rate against a demand forecast. Two things have never worked: (a) extending it to the third of revenue that is not rooms — F&B, spa, meetings, parking — because those streams live in the disconnected systems of Problem 1; and (b) forecasting when demand is driven by exogenous shocks (pandemics, tariffs, immigration enforcement, a war) that no booking curve contains.
- **Binding constraint:** For (a), the same integration constraint as Problem 1: only 32% of hotels integrate loyalty/engagement data with PMS and CRM, and only 15% use predictive models even for labor planning (#101). For (b), exogeneity: the signal that moves demand is not in the data the model is trained on.
- **Does AI remove the constraint?** For (b), the industry's own forecasters demonstrate the limit. STR/Tourism Economics' 2026 U.S. RevPAR forecast moved from +0.6% in February (#113) to +2.8% by June after four months of actuals ran at +4.0%, with international inbound and outbound assumptions revised in opposite directions and "ongoing conflict with Iran" cited as a live variable (#114) — and was reportedly raised again in August. When the professional forecast for a whole market swings by a factor of five inside six months, an individual hotel's ML pricing engine is learning a faster booking curve, not seeing the shock. That is the script-generation trap in RM form: better fit, same blindness. For (a), the AI revenue-management products marketed as "total revenue" (#103's vendor category) require the unified data layer most hotels do not have; AI is downstream of the integration constraint, not a substitute for it.
- **Evidence anyone is attempting real removal:** Skift Research's 2021 benchmark — 28% of hotels used any RMS, 10% excluding OTA-provided free tools (#115) — is the most recent independent adoption figure found; every 2025–2026 adoption claim found came from RMS vendors and was not used here. The one real productivity number found in the operations data is not AI-driven: room-attendant minutes per occupied room fell 4.3% year over year to Q1 2026 while wages rose 4.3%, and the report attributes it to scheduling discipline and labor deployment, not technology (#116).
- **Institutional-capability formulation:** Total revenue optimization becomes possible only once Problem 1's integration is done — at which point the compounding capability is an owned, cross-outlet demand and margin model per guest. For exogenous shocks, the formulation is not a better forecaster but a faster institutional response loop (scenario playbooks tied to leading indicators the hotel can actually observe — flight searches, visa data, group pace), which is an organizational design, not a model.
- **Verdict:** ☑ Automation in disguise. AI sharpens the forecast inside the booking curve; it does not remove exogeneity, and total-revenue optimization is blocked by the Problem 1 constraint rather than by modeling capability.

## Problem 4: Service quality depends on human labor that does not scale and turns over constantly

- **Why it was considered unsolvable:** Hotels sell human attention delivered in a physical space. Housekeeping, front desk, F&B and maintenance are dexterous, variable, in-person tasks. The labor pool that does them quits at extraordinary rates: the accommodation-and-food-services quits rate was 3.5% in July 2026 against 1.9% for all nonfarm employment (#118), and more than half of surveyed U.S. hotels reported being understaffed heading into the 2026 World Cup summer, with 70% raising wages to recruit (#117).
- **Binding constraint:** Physical dexterity and judgment in unstructured environments (making a king bed with a 2.5-metre duvet, cleaning around a guest's belongings), at a wage low enough that robots are not yet cheaper — a version of "expert attention doesn't scale," where the scarce thing is a human body doing variable physical work.
- **Does AI remove the constraint?** Not yet, and the deployed robotics evidence is explicitly augmentation. The best-documented U.S. deployment found — TechForce's TIM-E at a Homewood Suites in Del Mar — moves linen and waste bins between floors on a ~$150/day subscription so that housekeepers cleaning 16–18 rooms a day stop hauling "several hundred pounds"; its own maker says it "should really be a tool… augmenting staff rather than replacing them" (#119). Marriott's LG/RobotLAB pilots are delivery and concierge robots (#119, note). The only claimed fully robot-run hotel — Pudu Robotics' project on Shenzhen's West Artificial Island — is scheduled for limited trials in late 2026 and opening in 2027, with no disclosed staffing numbers (#120). A robotics researcher at Hotelschool The Hague argues housekeeping sits at "the very end" of the automation queue: deformable, heavy objects, speed requirements, and low wages that make the economics of a humanoid worst-first (#121). The productivity gains that *have* shown up in the data (#116) came from scheduling, not machines. AI is meanwhile absorbing the screen-based labor around the physical work — Airbnb resolves ~45% of support issues without a human, Booking's cost per booking is falling double-digit (#108, #112) — but that is the call center, not the guest room.
- **Evidence anyone is attempting real removal:** Pudu (#120) is the only found attempt at removal rather than augmentation, and it is unproven. Everything else in the evidence base (#119, #121) is a load-carrying or delivery robot next to an unchanged human housekeeping cycle.
- **Institutional-capability formulation:** If the dexterity constraint falls (humanoids that make beds at human speed and cost), the institutional capability is not "owning robots" — that will be purchasable — but the operating model that redesigns the room, the linen, and the service standard around a mixed human/robot crew, which no operator in this evidence base has built. Until then, the durable capability is on the labor side: the scheduling and deployment discipline that produced the 4.3% MPOR gain (#116) is organizational and compounds.
- **Verdict:** ☑ Automation in disguise (augmentation of an unchanged labor model) — with the constraint itself still standing. The falsification test is explicit: a Pudu-style property running at human-equivalent quality and cost in 2027 would move this to genuine invention. No such evidence exists as of 2026-09.

## Problem 5: The independent hotel's structural disadvantage against chains

- **Why it was considered unsolvable:** Chains have distribution scale (loyalty members, brand.com, negotiated OTA terms), technology scale (a central stack amortized over thousands of hotels), and data scale (Problem 1's graph). An independent has a building and a location. The gap has widened, not closed, through every previous technology cycle.
- **Binding constraint:** Scale economies in demand aggregation and data — coordination cost. Independents' global RevPAR fell 5.4% in 2025 while OTA share of their bookings rose to 63.4% (near 80% in some markets); nearly 80% of chains report using AI versus 41% of independents (#103). Roughly 1,900 U.S. hotels converted flags in 2025, the most since 2016, and in the most recent breakdown found about 35% of converting hotels went *independent* — but the chains launched conversion-purpose soft brands specifically to harvest that pool (#122).
- **Does AI remove the constraint?** The purchasable part of the gap is closing — cloud PMS, channel managers, and RMS at $79–149/month are available to any 30-room property (#103, #110 note) — and that is precisely why it is not a moat: the same Cloudbeds/Mews/SiteMinder stack is on sale to every competitor on the same street. The non-purchasable part is getting worse. Google's agentic booking launched with ten partners, all chains and OTAs (#110); the practical mechanics of being *seen* by an agent — real-time inventory sync, structured data, content parity across channels — favor whoever already has a central stack, so an independent reaches the agent through an OTA, which reinforces the 63.4% (#103). The "AI discovery" shift (generative-AI usage for travel planning more than doubled; traditional search use fell from 51% to 36%; OTAs appear in more than half of AI recommendations — #103) moves the aggregation point without moving who aggregates.
- **Evidence anyone is attempting real removal:** None found. Every vendor in the evidence base sells independents a better on-ramp to the existing aggregators. No consortium, cooperative, or shared merchant-of-record layer that would let independents present as a single agent-facing counterparty was found — despite the chains (#105, #106) and OTAs (#108) all building exactly that for themselves.
- **Institutional-capability formulation:** The constraint is scale in aggregation, and the only way an independent removes rather than rents around it is collective: a hotel-owned, agent-facing endpoint (inventory, content, identity, payment) operated by a cooperative or an existing rep company, with the members' combined scale negotiating placement in Google AI Mode and OpenAI's app layer the way a chain does. The agent protocols now standardizing (ACP, Google's partner APIs — #109, #110) make this technically cheaper than it has ever been; nobody in the evidence base is doing it.
- **Verdict:** ☑ Automation in disguise for the purchased stack (the baseline just rose for everyone). ☑ **Open opportunity** — flagged for problem solvers and investors — for a collective, hotel-owned agent-facing distribution layer for independents, the hospitality analogue of the ad-servers consortium proposal. Forward-looking; deliberately not validated against a market precedent, because none was found.

## Problem 6: Restaurant unit economics — is AI removing the labor constraint or shaving seconds?

- **Why it was considered unsolvable:** Restaurant labor runs roughly a third of sales and cannot be offshored; throughput is bounded by how fast humans assemble food in a fixed footprint. Every technology wave (POS, kiosks, apps) shifted work to the customer without touching the kitchen line.
- **Binding constraint:** Physical assembly throughput per labor-hour in the kitchen, and the accuracy of order capture at the drive-thru — both human, both variable.
- **Does AI remove the constraint?** Split answer, and the split is the finding. *Voice AI at the drive-thru* does not: the only independent measurement found — Intouch Insight's 2025 mystery-shop study across Taco Bell, Wendy's and Bojangles — put AI-only order accuracy at 83% against 89% for human order-takers, with employees intervening in roughly a quarter of AI interactions (#123). Operators have therefore kept a human "monitor" on the headset. Taco Bell has voice AI in 900+ restaurants (#124), McDonald's ArchIQ (Google) is at five test sites after the IBM effort was cancelled in 2024, Wendy's FreshAI expansion targets 500–600 stores, and Chipotle's Hyphen makeline and Autocado remain in pilot (#125). Vendor-reported accuracy and "containment" figures for all of these are labeled V in the log and not used as outcomes. Faster order capture with a human still in the loop is the shaving-seconds case. *Kitchen automation* is different in kind: Sweetgreen reports that established Infinite Kitchens deliver "more than 700 basis points in labor savings over classic locations of similar age" — that is a claim about the assembly constraint itself, not the order-capture layer — and roughly half of 2026 openings will be IK (#126). Two caveats are load-bearing. First, the figure is company-reported and Sweetgreen's overall business is deteriorating regardless of IK: comparable sales fell 11.5% in Q4 2025 and 12.8% in Q1 2026, and restaurant-level margin fell from 17.4% to 10.4%, then 10.0% (#126) — automation of assembly does not generate demand. Second, only 32 of 285 restaurants are IK, so the system-level effect is small.
- **Evidence anyone is attempting real removal:** Sweetgreen (#126) is the only operator in the evidence base attacking the assembly constraint at production scale, and it did so by acquiring the robotics company (Spyce) rather than purchasing a vendor product. Yum's Byte platform is the sharpest falsification case in the whole domain: its CFO states that "without a connected technology platform, the integration of Voice AI into restaurants would be significantly more challenging and costly," that owning the stack lets Yum "own the data," and that Byte should "power the vast majority of Yum! system sales outside of China" (#124). The voice-AI vendor (Omilia) is purchasable; the platform it plugs into is not.
- **Institutional-capability formulation:** The intelligence belongs upstream of the drive-thru speaker — in an owned ordering/kitchen/data platform (Byte) or an owned assembly system (Infinite Kitchen) — where every store's data compounds into the next store's design. Purchased voice AI on a franchisee's legacy POS is personal-productivity-grade automation at chain scale.
- **Verdict:** ☑ Automation in disguise for drive-thru voice AI (human monitor still required; independent accuracy below human). ☑ Genuine invention candidate for owned kitchen automation (Sweetgreen IK: a reported 700 bps against the actual assembly constraint) — provisional, because the reporting is company-sourced and the parent's economics are falling for unrelated demand reasons. Falsification check outcome: the one place a "purchased" automation looks durable (Taco Bell voice AI at 900 stores) turns out to depend on an *owned* platform (Byte), which is the framework's prediction, not an exception to it.

## Problem 7: Trust and safety in short-term rentals at platform scale

- **Why it was considered unsolvable:** A marketplace of millions of unlicensed, unstaffed homes has to police fraud (fake listings, phishing, fake damage claims), disruptive use (parties), and discrimination (hosts rejecting guests by perceived race) with no employee on site — and the platform is simultaneously the judge, the insurer, and the party with a revenue interest in every booking it lets through. Meanwhile cities have decided the legitimacy question by statute.
- **Binding constraint:** Two, stacked. Adversarial adaptation (fraudsters learn each detection signal — the same constraint as ad fraud), and a legitimacy constraint imposed from outside: regulators, not models, decide which listings may exist.
- **Does AI remove the constraint?** On the adversarial layer, AI is scaling both sides at once. Airbnb's anti-party model blocked 20,000+ U.S. and 3,000+ Canadian bookings for one New Year's Eve and the company reports a more-than-50% drop in the global rate of party reports since the 2020 ban (#127); its pre-publication screening blocked roughly 265,000 suspicious listings in 2025 (#128). But the same year, AI-generated photographs were used in a $16,000 damage claim by a Manhattan superhost, and Airbnb's response — a Terms of Service change effective 2026-04-20 banning "AI-generated, AI-enhanced, upscaled, or synthetic material of any kind" as evidence (#128) — is a policy rule, not a detector, which is what you write when detection can't keep up. On discrimination, the biggest measured improvement came from a product-design change, not a model: hiding guest profile photos until after acceptance closed about a fifth of the gap, and the Black–white booking-success disparity narrowed from 2.7 to 1.4 percentage points between 2021 and 2023 (#129); no AI claim is attached to it. On legitimacy, no model is relevant: New York's Local Law 18 removed more than 90% of listings (#130), Spain's registry sweep produced 65,122 non-compliant listings and a €64 million fine that the Madrid High Court refused to suspend in March 2026 (#130), and Chesky told investors that "policy is a risk that the company will manage forever" (#112).
- **Evidence anyone is attempting real removal:** Airbnb's own AI investment is real and is documented as the best in the category (#112, #127, #128) — but the outcome numbers are all self-reported and, as with the ad-fraud case, the reported gains sit beside evidence that the offense is adopting the same tools.
- **Institutional-capability formulation:** Removing the adversarial constraint would mean changing what gets paid for — verified identity and verified property state at check-in/check-out as a condition of the transaction, so fraud stops being profitable rather than being detected faster. Removing the legitimacy constraint is not an AI problem at all; it is a data-sharing and licensing regime (the EU's 2024/1028 registry model) in which the platform is a compliant reporter, not the arbiter.
- **Verdict:** ☑ Automation in disguise — a triage arms race the platform reports it is winning by its own numbers, with the strongest measured anti-discrimination gain coming from UX design and the largest supply changes coming from regulators. No evidence found of AI removing either binding constraint.

## Falsification check (domain-level)

Looked for evidence that purchased automation *is* the moat somewhere in hospitality. Findings:

- The three largest platforms (Booking, Expedia, Airbnb) all report double-digit customer-service cost-per-booking declines from AI in the same quarter (#108, #111, #112). Simultaneous, identical gains across all competitors are the definition of a rising baseline, not a moat.
- Hilton's 80%-plus direct share (#106) and Marriott's 68–75% member room-night share (#105) are the durable assets in lodging distribution; both predate the LLM cycle and are loyalty/inventory-control economics.
- The one candidate for "purchased and durable" — Taco Bell's 900-store voice AI — resolves in the framework's favor: the purchasable vendor component is only valuable because of Yum's owned Byte platform (#124).
- Sweetgreen's Infinite Kitchen is owned (via acquisition), not purchased, and its reported 700 bps is against the real constraint — but it has not rescued the parent's economics (#126).

No exception found. The domain's pattern matches ad servers and fashion: AI is the interface on assets (identity graphs, inventory control, owned platforms) that were built by other means.
